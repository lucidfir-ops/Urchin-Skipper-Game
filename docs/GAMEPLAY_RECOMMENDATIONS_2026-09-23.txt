URCHIN SKIPPER — MAKING THE EXISTING GAME MORE FUN
23 September 2026 · Proposals for the designer to choose from

My recommendation: spend the next pass on the satisfaction and variety of a
working day. The game already has plenty of boats, maps, hazards, crew stats and
economics. More systems will not necessarily make a normal ten-minute session
more enjoyable. The strongest part is already here: anticipate a float's drift,
bring the port side alongside, bring aboard a bag, and decide what to do next.

These proposals follow the DOCX's skipper-skill and believable-working-boat
vision. None of the gameplay proposals below was implemented in this TEMP.
The September 23 touch/portrait/audio work is a separate delivered change.
Priorities are design judgments from the current code, feedback and recordings;
they are not evidence from a large player study or a completed season audit.

START WITH THESE THREE

1. Make a good pickup feel unmistakably good.
   Existing: port-side approach, relative-speed limits, distinct bag/boarding
   actions, hook timing, deck bags, audio, contextual prompts and diver whistles.
   Improve their timing and clarity as one event. A float becomes reachable,
   the hook catches, the bag lands with weight, the visible deck gains a bag,
   and a short crew acknowledgment closes the sequence. In Easy, explain a
   failed attempt with one current reason: too fast relative to the float,
   wrong side, too far, or operation already underway. Avoid rapid message
   switching between two nearby divers. Keep Realistic's information limits.
   Small first pass: presentation and cue audit of ten ordinary pickups, with
   no change to handling, recovery distances or simulation timing.
   Success: a player can explain why the last pickup worked or failed without
   opening a help menu. Ordinary bags should remain satisfying after twenty.
   Bible: §§2, 3, 10, 19. High value; modest scope.

2. Turn the offload receipt into a short story about the day.
   Existing: settlement, costs, freshness, crew earnings, records and rival
   landings. Do not build a second economy or replace the detailed accounts.
   Lead with net money kept, then two or three factual highlights: the best
   bag, an especially productive stretch, time spent waiting for pickup, or
   value lost by missing offload. Show one relevant personal best or comparison
   with the skipper's own previous trips. Keep gross revenue visually distinct
   from profit; a big catch with a bad margin is a useful lesson.
   Small first pass: select three highlights from existing recorded facts.
   Add new tracking only for facts we actually decide to show. Never invent a
   cause such as "bad ground" when the result cannot establish that.
   Success: after a trip, the player has a specific idea to try tomorrow.
   Bible: §§12–14, 16. High value; modest scope.

3. Give each morning a clear, tempting choice using existing systems.
   Existing: buyer demand today/tomorrow, area openings, forecasts, current,
   travel costs, crew fatigue, offload timing and fifteen working maps.
   Bring the relevant facts together at departure: nearby familiar ground with
   more fishing time, a quality buyer worth a longer trip, or a slack-water
   window that suits today's crew. Describe known facts and uncertainty. The
   player still chooses their own goal; no mandatory quest list or arcade score.
   Small first pass: a compact departure brief using the existing buyer and
   forecast data, plus an optional personal goal such as "pay for the thruster."
   Success: two sensible players can choose different trips for defensible
   reasons, instead of repeatedly choosing the richest unlocked map.
   Bible: §§2, 7, 12, 13. High value; moderate scope.

NEXT: MAKE EXPERIENCE AND PEOPLE MATTER

4. Make knowing a ground feel like ownership of useful knowledge.
   Existing: charts, observations, persistent stock, terrain differences and
   equipment that changes information. Improve memory and retrieval rather
   than reveal hidden quality everywhere. A note should help remember where
   a good drift started, which tide gave access, and when a bed was last worked.
   First pass: show the age and origin of useful observations in the chart,
   and make a couple of personal marks easy to revisit. If a fact was not
   observed by the player, do not quietly add it to a Realistic chart.
   Success: after several trips, players return to a place because they learned
   something, not because a menu tells them it is the highest tier.
   Bible: §§4, 5, 18. High value; moderate scope.

5. Let crew differences show up in small, memorable moments.
   Existing: named portraits/bios, training, differing skills, current tolerance,
   fatigue, injuries, availability, shares and poaching. More stat columns are
   not my first choice. Use occasional concise dialogue tied to actual events:
   one person reports a useful observation, another is pleased with a clean
   run of recoveries, an exhausted diver asks for a sensible rest.
   First pass: a small authored set for a few existing crew, with cooldowns and
   event conditions. No chatter every bag, free hidden-ground radar, or random
   disasters that make careful play irrelevant.
   Success: players remember who they hired and why they like working together.
   Bible: §§14, 15, 17. Medium/high value; moderate writing and tuning scope.

6. Make one or two rivals recognizable before adding more traffic.
   Existing: persistent named teams, boats, preferred home grounds, crew
   loyalty/poaching, real shared-stock pressure and fleet results.
   Surface their established habits through sightings and a little radio/harbour
   context. Seeing a familiar rival should inform a decision about moving or
   staying. A rival result should invite comparison without implying the same
   boat, ground, weather and working hours as the player.
   First pass: consistent short introductions and results for a few teams,
   grounded in their real activity. Keep the mystery boat mysterious.
   Success: a player recognizes a boat and changes a plan for a reason.
   Bible: §§16, 17. Medium value; modest/moderate scope.

BALANCE AND POLISH BEFORE EXPANSION

7. Test whether every upgrade creates a new way to work.
   Existing: different propulsion, draft, capacity, fuel costs and equipment.
   Give each purchase a plain example: "This lets me turn alongside floats in
   a tighter space" or "This makes that shallow access window usable."
   Compare an upgrade against keeping the cheaper boat with better crew and
   reserve cash. If one purchase is always correct, tune its tradeoffs before
   adding another near-identical hull. Preserve the confirmed handling baseline.
   First pass: replay a few identical conditions with current boats and record
   earnings, fuel, recovery workload and access; revise only with designer buy-in.
   Success: at least two viable upgrade paths that feel different to skipper.
   Bible: §§6, 13, 14. High balance value; measurement first.

8. Protect the rhythm: productive work, a decision, then a consequence.
   The tablet recording shows the basic repeat-bag loop already functioning.
   Measure how often players wait without anything interesting to judge, and
   how often two divers, traffic and notices demand attention simultaneously.
   More hazards or faster clocks can increase stress without increasing fun.
   First pass: observe three comparable trips—calm familiar water, one current
   challenge, and a longer quality-focused run. Log time spent working, waiting,
   navigating menus and repositioning. Look for dull stretches and overload.
   Success: quiet moments allow planning; busy moments reward preparation.
   Bible: §§2, 7–10, 17. High value; do this before broad timing changes.

9. Make setbacks teach, while leaving a credible recovery path.
   Existing: repair costs, injuries/absence history, fatigue, insurance, dock
   work, delayed offload and freshness losses. Explain the actual sequence of
   causes after a bad day. Let the player discover a sensible next step from
   existing options, such as resting, hiring a replacement or working the dock.
   First pass: improve the explanation of one common disappointing outcome.
   Do not erase serious consequences, add arbitrary bailouts, or rework saves.
   Success: "I understand what happened and what to try" beats "the game just
   took my money." Repeated failure should not require reading the source.
   Bible: §§13–15. Medium/high value; modest scope.

10. Use atmosphere and rare surprises sparingly.
    Existing weather, wildlife, procedural audio and mystery-boat material are
    enough for a focused polish pass. Better transitions between engine, water,
    wind and brief radio cues would contribute more than frequent new events.
    Save unusual readings or distant lights for genuinely rare moments; they
    should not obscure floats, imply false actionable safety information, or
    become a reward grind. Physical audio testing comes before new sound layers.
    First pass: make the current mix readable at tablet-speaker volume and
    verify that repeated engine loops do not dominate everything else.
    Success: the coast has character and useful sound without constant chatter.
    Bible: §§3, 7, 17. Medium value; polish before new content.

WHAT I WOULD DEFER

- More maps, nearly identical boats and extra equipment statistics until the
  existing choices are distinct and the early career has a good pace.
- Multiplayer, manual underwater control, an elaborate mission system or a
  second scoring/progression currency. Some would need a design amendment;
  all would distract from the current skipper experience.
- Extra random penalties or tighter recovery tolerances as a substitute for
  meaningful difficulty. Current, visibility, crew and boat differences already
  offer a strong foundation.
- A complete UI redesign. Finish the device fixes and observe real trips first.

SUGGESTED DESIGNER SORTING

Mark each numbered proposal YES / LATER / NO, then pick one small pass. My first
choice is pickup feedback plus a concise end-of-day debrief, followed by the
morning decision brief. Those improvements reinforce the game already built.
